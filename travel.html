neon lights and grand canyon views - dec '25

This trip was never supposed to look like this. We had a prebooked December getaway to Jamaica right after my brother and I wrapped up finals week — something tropical and restorative after a long semester. Yet, just a few weeks before we were set to leave, Hurricane Melissa hit, and the country shifted its focus to rebuilding. It was the right call to cancel, but that did not make the disappointment any smaller. Expectations were high, and suddenly we were reworking plans while already exhausted.

Pivoting to Las Vegas for the third time felt almost painful at first. Familiarity can dull excitement, especially when you are still holding onto what a trip was meant to be. We reframed the plan entirely. Two days in Vegas using my mom’s Hilton package, then the rest of the trip devoted to something we had somehow missed for years. The Grand Canyon.

Vegas became a starting point rather than the destination. Since the Strip felt repetitive, we focused on experiences we had not done before. We visited the Arte Museum, which ended up feeling like a weaker version of teamLab in Tokyo and not something I would recommend. In contrast, Ka by Cirque du Soleil delivered exactly what Vegas does best. Immersive, dramatic, and worth seeing once. Those two days felt contained and intentional, a pause before the trip shifted direction.

From there, we drove to Williams, Arizona, where the tone of the trip changed entirely. We boarded the Grand Canyon Express, and from the moment the train pulled away, everything slowed down. The ride itself was lovely, the kind of experience that reminds you travel does not always have to be about speed. Our train attendant had worked with the Grand Canyon (first the park, and now the Railway) since 1991 and shared stories from decades of guiding visitors through this landscape. She had even been a Fred Harvey Girl in the past, a detail that made the history feel personal rather than distant.

At the South Rim, we had lunch at the Fred Harvey House overlooking the canyon, an experience that felt both grounding and quietly special. The museum inside offered context to the legacy of the Harvey Girls and the role they played in shaping early travel in the American West. I was especially taken by the designs of Mary Colter, whose architectural vision blends seamlessly into the landscape. Even the crockery at the Fred Harvey House is original, a small but meaningful reminder of how much care went into preserving this place.

Seeing the Grand Canyon for the first time was surreal. I had been to Antelope Canyon and Bryce Canyon six years earlier, yet somehow never made it here. Standing at the rim, the scale was impossible to fully process. It felt vast, patient, and completely indifferent to the fact that this trip existed because another one fell apart.

Afterward, we drove to Phoenix to fly home, the road long and open in a way that invited reflection. This trip taught me something I keep relearning. Not every journey begins with excitement. Some start with disappointment, detours, and lowered expectations. But sometimes those are the trips that leave the deepest impression.

What stayed with me was not Vegas or even the Canyon alone, but the way the trip asked us to adapt. To let go of what we thought we wanted and stay open to what was still possible. In the end, neon lights gave way to canyon views, and a trip that almost did not happen became one defined by history, patience, and quiet awe.

central europe highlights - aug ’25

This summer, I traveled with my grandparents and family on our first multi-country trip through Central Europe, a 10-day journey that felt both special and overdue. I usually travel fast. My itineraries are dense and optimized, almost competitive. I like knowing I have seen everything I was supposed to see. Travel, for me, has often felt like a checklist.

This trip forced me to unlearn that. My grandparents move at a slower pace now. Long walking days are not realistic, and plans have to bend around rest, benches, cafés, and train schedules that do not revolve around rushing. At first, it felt like something was being taken away. Fewer sights, fewer steps, fewer boxes checked. Over time, the slower rhythm began to feel intentional rather than limiting.

We began in Prague, arriving by train, already one of my favorite parts of traveling in Europe. Watching the countryside blur past the window felt like a transition rather than just a commute. In Prague, cobblestone streets, pastel Baroque buildings, and the constant scent of chimney cakes set the tone. We explored Prague Castle and St. Vitus Cathedral, watched the Astronomical Clock strike the hour, and floated down the Vltava on a river cruise beneath the Charles Bridge. Instead of trying to cover everything, we wandered. We sat. We watched the city move around us. It felt cinematic in a quiet, unforced way.

From there, another train carried us to Vienna, a city that feels curated and precise, almost like an open-air museum. We toured the Hofburg Palace, including the Sisi Museum and Imperial Apartments, and spent time in the Austrian National Library, whose scale and symmetry stopped me in my tracks. Walks through Stadtpark led us to the golden Strauss monument, and afternoons naturally slowed into coffee and cake. Sachertorte at Café Central was not just dessert. It was an excuse to sit longer, talk more, and let the day breathe.

Budapest was where the trip began to feel more layered. The city came alive at sunset, with the Hungarian Parliament Building glowing along the Danube and Fisherman’s Bastion and Matthias Church offering sweeping views from above. We walked through Heroes’ Square, wandered around Vajdahunyad Castle, and spent time café hopping on the Pest side. One evening, my brother and I split off from the rest of the family for the first time on the trip. It felt quietly important, realizing that traveling together does not mean doing everything together.

That night, we ended up at Fat Mama, where I had Hungarian chicken paprikásh that was genuinely unforgettable. Sitting there with just my brother made me realize how travel can hold multiple experiences at once. Family time and independence. Togetherness and space. It did not fracture the trip. It made it richer.

A half-day trip to Szentendre brought colorful art streets, ceramic studios, and a calmer riverside pace. Back in Budapest, we climbed Gellért Hill at dusk and watched the city slowly light up bridge by bridge.

We returned to Prague for our final night by train, crossing Charles Bridge one last time before saying goodbye over coffee at Café Savoy. It felt full-circle in a way I did not expect. I know I will come back to these cities. My grandparents probably will not. That quiet truth stayed with me throughout the trip.

Travel is not just about how much you see. It is about timing, people, and learning when to slow down. This trip taught me that a relaxed itinerary does not mean a diminished experience. It can mean a deeper one. What I will remember most is not the number of landmarks we visited, but the space between them. The train rides, the shared meals, and the understanding that traveling together does not require moving through the world in the same way.

vietnam, thailand and cambodia - may '25

I joined a solo travelers’ backpacking group for two weeks, stepping into a version of travel that felt unfamiliar and grounding at the same time. Being on the road with strangers strips things down quickly. There is no shared history, no fixed dynamic, just people moving together for a short stretch of time. We started in Bangkok, where days unfolded through cruising the Chao Phraya River, wandering Chinatown, getting lost on local buses, and lingering over street Pad Thai and Thai tea. Visiting Wat Pho and day-tripping to Ayutthaya added moments of stillness and scale. Somewhere between stops, fresh rambutan and mangosteen became part of the daily rhythm, small comforts in a city that never really slows.

Cambodia was the part of the trip that stayed with me the most. I spent nine days there, moving between the quiet enormity of Angkor Wat near Siem Reap and the emotional weight of Phnom Penh. A 4am wakeup for sunrise at Angkor Wat felt almost ceremonial, the temples emerging slowly from darkness. Later, visits to S-21 and the Killing Fields were sobering in a way that demanded time and silence. There were moments of contrast throughout, like trying chili-fried crickets in Phnom Penh, unwinding on the beaches of Koh Rong, and hiking up to Chambok Canyon, whose beauty felt almost unreal in its calm.

What warmed me most about Cambodia, though, was the people. Our tour guide, Kosal, was a local from Siem Reap, and over the course of the trip, his story quietly reshaped how I understood everything around me. He spent his childhood helping his parents fish and farm, earning a living instead of going to school until he was nineteen. Eventually, he enrolled in English classes through New Hope, where he learned the language that later opened doors for him. That path led him to meet his wife, build a life, and work as a tour guide. He spoke often about his children and his insistence on sending them to private English school, not out of status, but out of conviction in what education can change.

We visited The New Hope Vocational Training Restaurant, an initiative kickstarted by G Adventures that provides skill-building opportunities to marginalized community members. The project also funds an adjacent free community school and health center, both run by locals. Eating there felt different. The food was excellent, but the context mattered more. Later, we shared a meal at Phila’s house, where proceeds support the education of underprivileged children in the area. These were not abstract ideas about impact or development. They were lived, immediate, and deeply human.

One night in Chambok, I stayed with a local farming community, another reminder that the most grounding travel moments often come without spectacle. I also visited a traditional two-story straw cottage in the countryside, where lunch was served on the floor. Noodles and lemongrass-coconut chicken never tasted better, not because of novelty, but because of the care and intention behind the meal.

After returning home, I told a professor at my university about the trip. He mentioned that he had traveled to Cambodia around the same age, and that it stayed with him decades later. What he remembered most was his tour guide, who was his age and not in school. Hearing that made something click. It explained why Kosal’s story lingered with me, and why Cambodia felt heavier and more meaningful than I had expected. Seeing someone so close in age, shaped by a completely different set of circumstances, reframed the experience in a way I am still processing.

After Cambodia, I spent three days in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, easing back into motion and noise. I shopped nonstop at Ben Thanh Market, Saigon Square, and Takashimaya, and wandered Book Street when I needed a pause. The War Remnants Museum was sobering in a quieter way, while comfort came through food and routine. Egg coffee at Little Hanoi, banana pancakes with extra Sữa Ông Thọ made by my hostel hosts, and the small victory of crossing the road without getting run over all felt earned. I was especially proud of navigating an hour-long local bus ride to the airport on my own.

Looking back, this trip mattered not because of how much I saw, but because of when I saw it. Some experiences imprint themselves because they meet you at the right moment in life. Cambodia did that for me. I do not yet know how it will shape the way I move through the world, only that years from now, I suspect it will still be part of how I understand it.

japan - mar '25

I spent my 20th birthday week in Japan, averaging over 30,000 steps a day, eight Shinkansen rides, and twenty-two local trains. Looking back, it felt like a deliberate kind of motion, as if I was testing how much I could take on and still feel present. It was fast, imperfect, exhilarating, and deeply formative.

I landed in Osaka and almost immediately met up with strangers I had connected with through an online forum. What could have felt risky or awkward turned into one of the highlights of the trip. We wandered the city together, laughing over shared confusion and late-night plans, and it struck me how easily travel collapses distance between people. Osaka felt generous and alive. I took a day trip to Nara to watch deer roam freely, savor matcha ice cream, and explore Todai-ji. Evenings buzzed with the energy of the Namba markets, where I devoured octopus takoyaki and felt, for the first time, fully untethered in a new place.

The next day, I caught the Shinkansen to Hiroshima, then a ferry to Miyajima. Along the way, I squeezed in thrift shopping at Mode-Off and stocked up on discounted skincare at Matsumoto Kiyoshi, small rituals that made the trip feel my own. I accidentally fell asleep on a bus and ended up in Waratenjinmae, a detour that rerouted my plans and forced me to let go of control. Because of it, I missed seeing Kinkaku-ji, something that frustrated me in the moment but later felt like an early lesson in patience. It gave me a reason to come back.

Hiroshima was deeply moving. The Peace Memorial Park and Museum left a quiet heaviness that stayed with me long after I left. I ate Hiroshima-style okonomiyaki, watched the sunrise over Hiroshima Castle with melon bread from 7-Eleven, and later stood on Miyajima Island as the tide rose around the floating torii gate at Itsukushima Shrine.

Kyoto offered a different kind of stillness. Walking through the bamboo forest felt almost unreal in its calm. I did not see Kinkaku-ji on this trip, and Mount Fuji remained hidden behind thick fog, both reminders that some things are meant to be unfinished. Instead of disappointment, I left with intention. There are places you return to not because you missed them, but because you are meant to meet them later.

By day four, I was in Tokyo, moving with more confidence than I had at the start of the trip. I explored Asakusa and Senso-ji Temple, wandered through Ueno Park, and shopped without restraint. Nakano Broadway pulled me into its maze of vintage toys and niche treasures. A bowl of ramen in Harajuku fueled an evening in Shibuya, where I stood at the crossing watching the city surge around me before ending the night with late-night sushi.

Turning twenty in Japan felt symbolic in a way I did not fully grasp at the time. This was the trip where independence stopped feeling theoretical. Navigating trains, meeting strangers, getting lost, missing landmarks, and finding my way again taught me that growing up does not arrive all at once. It accumulates in moments like these. Japan did not just introduce me to travel. It showed me the version of myself that travel would continue to reveal.
